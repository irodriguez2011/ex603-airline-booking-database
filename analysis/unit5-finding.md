**To:** Leadership Team
**Subject:** Two Different Flight Problems, Two Different Fixes

Our booking data shows two separate groups of flights that need attention. They look similar on a dashboard, because both are "underperforming", but they have different causes and need different responses. Treating them the same way would waste effort on both.

**Group 1: Flights that are booked but fail often**

These flights sell seats, but too many of those bookings end badly. Across the platform, 6% of bookings end in a passenger no-show. Among our busiest flights, the normal rate is 4.8%.

- **VN202:** 3 no-shows out of 16 bookings (18.8%), nearly four times the normal rate.
- **VN203:** 2 no-shows and 2 disputed bookings out of 13. It is also listed in our system as inactive, even though it carried 13 bookings.
- **VN211:** its no-show rate is normal, but 5 of its 16 bookings (31%) were cancelled. Three of those were not the passenger's choice: two were flights we cancelled and one was a failed payment.

There is a pattern behind the first two. Our five 320-seat flights (VN201 to VN205) have a no-show rate of 10.9%, nearly three times the 3.7% on all our other flights. The no-shows come from many different passengers, so this is not one problem traveler.

**Recommended action for Group 1: fix how these flights operate, and measure the result.** Start check-in reminders and seat confirmations 24 hours before departure on the 320-seat flights, beginning with VN202 and VN203. Separately, have operations review why VN211 was cancelled twice by us and why a payment failed. Correct VN203's status so it is no longer listed as inactive. After one quarter, compare each flight's no-show rate with the 4.8% benchmark to see whether the changes worked.

**Group 2: Flights that never started**

**VN214** (a 303-seat aircraft) and **VN215** (a 132-seat aircraft) did not receive a single booking in all of 2023. Both are still listed as active. Neither one appeared in any earlier report, because those reports only looked at flights that had bookings.

Neither flight has a route assigned in our system: no airports are attached to them. That may mean passengers could never find them to book. We cannot confirm this from the data alone, though, because one other flight (VN213) also has no route and still received bookings.

**Recommended action for Group 2: find out whether these flights were ever on sale, then decide their future.** Within the next two weeks, ask the scheduling team to confirm whether VN214 and VN215 were ever offered to customers. If they were never set up properly, finish the setup and open them for sale. If they were on sale and nobody booked them, decide whether to promote them, move the aircraft to a busier route, or retire the flights so they stop counting as active capacity. Reminders and check-in changes will not help here. These flights have no passengers to remind.

**Why the distinction matters**

Group 1 has customers and loses some of them. The fix is operational, and we can measure it against the 4.8% benchmark. Group 2 has no customers at all. That is a setup or demand question, and the right answer may be to stop running the flight. VN214 alone is one of the largest aircraft in our fleet earning nothing, so Group 2 is the more urgent question even though it is the smaller group.
